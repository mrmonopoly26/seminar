\section{Architectural comparison of \acp{SNN} and \acp{ANN}}\label{Sec:ArchitecturalComparisonOfSNNsAndANNs}
In this section, \acp{SNN} and \acp{ANN} are compared regarding their core components.
This encompasses the underlying neural models, the associated signal propagation and activation mechanisms and the learning strategies.

\subsection{Neural models}\label{Subsec:NeuralModels}
\acp{SNN} and \acp{ANN} both are inspired by biological neurons.
Fig.~\ref{fig:bioneuron} illustrates the basic anatomy of a biological neuron, which is composed of four principal components: \textit{dendrites}, \textit{soma}, \textit{axon}, and \textit{synapse}.  
Dendrites function as the input interface of the neuron by converting chemical signals from neurotransmitters into electrical signals.  
These signals are then integrated in the soma, the cell body, where the accumulation of membrane potentials determines whether an action potential is generated.  
This outcome of this process, known as neural integration, decides whether the neuron will fire.  
If the threshold is reached, the neuron emits a spike and the spike is transmitted via the axon to other neurons.  
The synapses are the structural junctions that enable the communication between neurons and are typically located on dendrites, facilitating the transformation and relay of signals within the neural network~\cite{yamazaki_spiking_2022, pietrzak_overview_2023}.  
\acp{SNN} and \acp{ANN} model this structure in similar ways.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includesvg[inkscapelatex=false, width=0.45\textwidth]{figs/bio_neuron}
    \caption[The structure of a biological neuron.]{The structure of a biological neuron.
    Dendrites receive incoming signals and transmit them to the soma for integration.
    If triggered, an action potential travels along the axon to the synapses, where it is relayed to other neurons (figure inspired by ~\cite{yamazaki_spiking_2022}).}
    \label{fig:bioneuron}
\end{figure}

A neuron in an \ac{ANN} can be considered as a mathematical function.
The function multiplies input features \( x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n \) with corresponding weights \( w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n \).
The results are aggregated (with a bias term) and transformed by an activation function to produce a numeric output.
The activation function introduces non-linearity into the model, which is crucial for learning complex patterns~\cite{taye_understanding_2023}.
A neuron can be formalized as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y} = \sigma(w_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i \cdot x_i)
    \label{annneuronequation}
\end{equation}
\(\hat{y}\) is the output of the neuron, \(\sigma\) is the activation function and \(w_0\) is the bias term.

A single neuron is insufficient to solve complex tasks.
Therefore, multiple neurons are organized into layers to form a network, where each neuron processes part of the input and contributes to the overall output.
Networks typically consist of an input layer, hidden layers and an output layer.
Networks with many hidden layers are referred to as deep neural networks and are particularly suited to capturing high-level data abstractions~\cite{taye_understanding_2023}.
The term \textit{network} is derived from the structure of these computations.
For example in feedforward neural networks, a special type of \acp{ANN}, information flows in one direction from input to output without any cycles.
Such networks can be understood as compositions of multiple functions \( f^{(1)}, f^{(2)}, \ldots, f^{(n)} \) forming an acyclic graph~\cite[p.~164]{goodfellow_deep_2016}, which is similar to a network.
Goodfellow et al. claim that the field of \acp{ANN} is nowadays driven by engineering and mathematical disciplines rather than neuroscience.
Although the original design of \acp{ANN} was inspired by the brain, modern \acp{ANN} primarily serve as statistical function approximators, optimized for generalization rather than for emulating biological processes~\cite[p.~165]{goodfellow_deep_2016}.
Nevertheless, the components can be meaningfully compared.
The input features correspond to the incoming electrical signals of a biological neuron.
Aggregation and activation resemble the processes taking place within the soma.
The numerical output represents the outgoing signal.

\acp{SNN} as more plausible and realistic models of biological neurons, were introduced by Maass.
They constitute a distinct class of artificial neural networks in which information is transmitted via sequences of discrete time-dependent events known as \textit{spikes} or \textit{spike trains} in contrast to the numeric real-valued activations used in \acp{ANN}~\cite{maass_networks_1997}.
This implies that in \acp{SNN} information is encoded in the timing of spikes rather than in their shape or intensity, allowing them to more closely mimic biological neurons~\cite{ponulak2_introduction_2011, maass_networks_1997}.
In the literature a spike train is often defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
    S(t) = \sum_f \delta(t - t_f).
    \label{diracdelta}
\end{equation}
\( \delta(\cdot) \) denotes the Dirac delta function and \( t_f \) are the firing times with the labels \( f = 1, 2, ... \).
The Dirac delta function resembles a distribution with \( \delta(0) \neq 0\) for \( t = 0 \) with \(\int_{-\infty  }^{\infty}\delta(t)dt = 1\)~\cite{ponulak2_introduction_2011, Gerstner}.
This indicates a spike exclusively at \( t = 0 \) and the signal being zero for \( t \neq 0\).
The Dirac delta function is not entirely unproblematic.
Since it is non-differentiable~\cite{tavanaei_deep_2019} and theoretically infinitesimal, it is often approximated in practice.
Different functions are used for this approximation, for example gaussian functions:
\begin{equation}
      h(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi a}} e^{\frac{(u - V_{th})^2}{2a}}.
      \label{gaussianapprox}
\end{equation}
% TODO cite wu
Here, \(u(t)\) denotes the membrane potential at time \(t\), a is a coefficient \(a\) and \(V_{th}\) is the firing threshold~\cite{Wu}.
Fig.~\ref{fig:spiketrain} shows such an example spike train created with a gaussian function.
\begin{figure}

    \includesvg[width=0.475\textwidth, inkscapelatex=false]{figs/spike_train}
    \caption[A spike train.]{A spike train. The spikes are modeled by the gaussian function from Eq.~\ref{gaussianapprox} with coefficient \(a=0.1\). The spikes are emitted at time \( t=3,7,8 \).}
    \label{fig:spiketrain}
\end{figure}

There are several \ac{SNN} neuron models, varying in biological realism and computational complexity using spike trains as core components.
The \ac{IF} model is the most basic \ac{SNN} model.
It abstracts the neuron as a system that integrates incoming spikes over time.
If the membrane potential reaches the firing threshold, it emits spike ~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023, nunes_spiking_2022}.
% TODO add formulas for IF and LIF
The \ac{IF} model can be described as first-order differential equation (following the convention of ~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023}):
\begin{equation}
    C_m\frac{dV}{dt}=I(t).
    \label{equ:if}
\end{equation}
\(C_m\) denotes to the membrane capacitance, \(\frac{dV}{dt}\) describes the rate of change of the membrane potential over time and \(I(t)\) is the integrated input current of other neurons at time \(t\).

The \ac{LIF} model extends the \ac{IF} model by including a leak term that causes the membrane potential to decay over time.
This makes the model biologically more realistic~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023, nunes_spiking_2022}:
\begin{equation}
    C_m\frac{dV}{dt}=I(t)-g_L(V(t)-E_L).
    \label{equ:lif}
\end{equation}
The only difference compared to Eq.~\ref{equ:if} is the leakage term \(-g_L(V(t)-E_L)\).
Here, \(g_L\) is the leak conductance, \(V(t)\) is the current membrane potential and \(E_L\) is the resting potential~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023}.
The leakage term models a leakage of membrane potential over time.
This means that the membrane potential is set back to a resting potential in the absence of incoming spikes.

Suppose the following: 
A neuron receives no input current \(V(t)=0\).
Therefore Eq.~\ref{equ:lif} simplifies to \(C_m\frac{dV}{dt}=-g_L(V(t)-E_L)\):
\begin{itemize}
    \item If \(V(t) > E_L\), the leakage term is \(-g_L(V(t)-E_L) < 0\), which implies that \(V(t)\) decreases.
    \item If \(V(t) = E_L\), the leakate term is \(-g_L(V(t)-E_L) = 0\), which implies no change of \(V(t)\).
\end{itemize}
An example for the change of the membrane potential of a \ac{LIF} neuron is plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:membranepotentiallif}.
Due to the simplicity and low computational costs compared to other neuron models, the \ac{LIF} model and its variants are widely used~\cite{yamazaki_spiking_2022}.

A biologically more plausible model is the Izhikevich model.
It captures a broader range of spiking behaviors (for example bursting or chattering) compared to the \ac{LIF} model, while remaining computationally efficient~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023}.
The most realistic neuron model is the Hodgkin-Huxley model.
It has been developed due to findings from an experiment with a squid axon.
Since it has been directly derived from biological processes, it has the highest fidelity of all neuron models.
However, it is computational expensive, making it infeasible for large-scale simulations~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023}.
There are several other neuron models which differ in terms of complexity and biological plausibility. 
Therefore, choosing an appropriate model for a specific application often leads to trade-off between these two criteria~\cite{yamazaki_spiking_2022}.


\subsection{Signal propagation and activation mechanisms} \label{Subsec:SignalPropagationAndActivationMechanisms}
This section explains and compares signal propagation and activation mechanisms.
Signal propagation means, how information is transmitted through the network.
Activation mechanisms describe the reason for neurons emitting a signal.

In \acp{ANN}, information is propagated as continuous real-valued signals.
Each neuron receives an input vector and produces a single output value. 
In Feed Forward Networks for example, neurons in the hidden or output layers take the outputs of other neurons in previous layers as their inputs~\cite[p.~164]{goodfellow_deep_2016}.
The inputs are multiplied by associated weights, summed, and then transformed by an activation function according to Eq.~\ref{annneuronequation}. 
Common activation functions include the sigmoid, tanh, \ac{ReLU} and SoftMax functions (especially for output layers)~\cite{taye_understanding_2023}. 

Signal propagation and activation are more complex in \acp{SNN}.
The information in \acp{SNN} is propagated by spike trains over time.
Due to the temporal dependency, input signals have to be encoded as spikes to retain relations between data points~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023}.
Before explaining the signal propagation and activation mechanisms, encoding schemas are introduced.

Pietrzak et al. and Nunes et al. outlined the two main encoding schemas:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rate encoding
    \item Temporal encoding
\end{itemize}
\textit{Rate encoding} represents information using the average number of spikes within a specific time interval.
These spikes can either be distributed evenly across the time axis or follow a Poisson process, the latter being more biologically plausible.
Rate encoding has been applied effectively in practical scenarios.
Nevertheless, focusing solely on average firing rates is not ideal~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023,nunes_spiking_2022}.

\textit{Temporal encoding} is based on timing of certain spikes rather than the firing rate over an interval.
For this case, one-spike-per-neuron networks are often used (a neuron emits only a single spike during an interval). 
This means, that the time delay of a spike encodes information sent by a neuron.
Considering a pixel value of 55 in a range of 0 until 255 and a time interval of 512 milliseconds:
the pixel value can be encoded as a spike emitted after \(\frac{(range-pixel)*{interval}}{range}=\frac{(256-55)*512}{256}=402\) milliseconds.
Temporal encoding is very efficient~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023} and is similar to biological processes~\cite{maass_networks_1997}.

All spiking models have in common, that the spike trains are integrated similar to the processes inside the soma. 
Fig.~\ref{fig:simplifiedactivation} illustrates a simplified example of a postsynaptic neuron emitting spike as a result of the integration of incoming spikes sent by presynaptic neurons.
In simple terms, \textit{presynaptic} neurons are the neurons which trigger the spikes.
The \textit{postsynaptic} neurons are the receivers of the spikes.

Similar to the variety of neural models, there are several possible activation mechanisms.
Maass described the activation of a neuron \(v\) for the \ac{IF} model as firing whenever the neuron's membrane potential \(P_v\) reaches a threshold \(\theta_v\).
The membrane potential is the sum of postsynaptic potentials from firing events of other neurons which are connected to this neuron.
The contribution to the potential of a neuron \(v\) at time \(t\) of a firing event of a neuron \(u\) at time \(s\) can be modeled by a term \(w_{u,v}*\varepsilon_{u,v}(t-s)\).
The parameter \(w_{u,v} \ge 0\) denotes the weight between the neurons, \(\varepsilon_{u,v}\) denotes a response function~\cite{maass_networks_1997}.
The weight is similar to the weights in \acp{ANN} describing the impact of a certain neuron \(u\) on neuron \(v\). 
The response function models the impact of a spike event from neuron \(u\) at time \(s\) on neuron \(v\) at time \(t\).
Depending on whether a synapse is excitatory (increasing the potential) or inhibitory (decreasing the potential), the response function is either \(\varepsilon_{u,v}\ge0\) or \(\varepsilon_{u,v}\le0\)~\cite{maass_networks_1997}.
Fig.~\ref{fig:responsefunctions} illustrates two biological plausible shapes for these response functions.

\subsection{Learning}
In simple terms, learning refers to weight changes in the context of neural networks, which is reflected both in \acp{ANN} and in \acp{SNN}.

Learning in \acp{ANN} is based on gradient descent algorithms~\cite{maass_networks_1997}.
The goal is to reduce a loss function by iteratively tweaking the weights in a network~\cite{taye_understanding_2023}.
To determine the loss, the network maps an input to an output in a forward pass.
A desired output is then compared to the output of the network~\cite{esser_backpropagation_2015}.
Using gradient descent, a weight parameter \(w\) can be updated by the following rule (adapted from~\cite[p.83]{goodfellow_deep_2016}):
\begin{equation}
      w' = w - \epsilon\nabla_xf(x),
\end{equation}
where \(w'\) denotes the updated weight parameter, \(\epsilon\) is the learning rate and \(\nabla_xf(x)\) represents the gradient of \(f(x)\).
In this context, the \textit{backpropagation} algorithm is commonly used.
Goodfellow et al. argue that backpropagation is often misunderstood, because it does not refer to the entire learning algorithm.
Instead, it specifically describes the method for efficiently computing the gradient of a cost function based on the chain rule.
Learning algorithms, such as stochastic gradient descent, use the computed gradients to change the weights in a network~\cite[p.200ff.]{goodfellow_deep_2016}.

The learning approaches for \acp{SNN} can be categorized in one of the following~\cite{yamazaki_spiking_2022, pietrzak_overview_2023, nunes_spiking_2022}: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \ac{ANN}-\ac{SNN} conversion
    \item Backpropagation / gradient based
    \item \ac{STDP}.
\end{itemize}

\textit{\ac{ANN}-\ac{SNN} conversion} is an indirect learning approach, which means that learning does not take place in the target \ac{SNN}~\cite{nunes_spiking_2022}.
The \ac{SNN} weights and other parameters are determined by training an \ac{ANN}.
The main idea is to map analogous values of the activation functions of the \ac{ANN} to firing rates or the average postsynaptic potentials of the spiking neurons.
The \ac{SNN} should achieve the same input-output mappings as the \ac{ANN}~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023}.
Pietrzak et al. divide the specific methods in two groups: \textit{regular conversion} and \textit{constrain-then-train conversion}.
For regular conversion an \ac{ANN} is trained once and can be converted into different \acp{SNN} with different parameters (neuron types, activation thresholds, etc.) afterwards.
Constrain-then-train conversion aims to use the \ac{SNN} on specific hardware and set of parameters~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023}.

Esser et al. demonstrate how a \textit{constrain-then-train} approach can be used to deploy a neural network on IBM's TrueNorth neuromorphic chip.  
In this method, an \ac{ANN} is trained using backpropagation and gradient descent, using the same network topology as the target \ac{SNN}.   
Inputs, neuron activations, and synaptic connection (the weights) in the training network are represented as continuous values in the range of \([0, 1]\).
They are interpreted as probabilities of spike occurrence or synapse activation.  
To convert the trained \ac{ANN} into the \ac{SNN}, the authors use a probabilistic sampling procedure in which each parameter is converted into a binary variable based on its probability.  
Multiple independently sampled networks are combined into an ensemble to improve the accuracy~\cite{esser_backpropagation_2015}.

\textit{Backpropagation} or \textit{gradient-based} learning methods employ mechanisms analogous to those used in the training of \acp{ANN}.  
However, these methods cannot be applied directly to \acp{SNN}.  
From a technical perspective, a key challenge lies in the fact that \acp{SNN} rely on spike trains typically modeled by the Dirac delta function.
This function is non-differentiable and thus incompatible with gradient descent techniques~\cite{tavanaei_deep_2019, pietrzak_overview_2023}.  
From a biological perspective, the so-called \textit{weight transport problem} presents another drawback.
Backpropagation assumes symmetric weights for the forward and backward passes, which does not hold in biological neural networks~\cite{tavanaei_deep_2019, nunes_spiking_2022}.
Despite these downsides, backpropagation or gradient-based methods can be applied to \acp{SNN} using approximations~\cite{tavanaei_deep_2019}.
SpikeProp, introduced by Bohte et al., is an early attempt to apply gradient-based learning to \acp{SNN}.  
The core idea is to approximate the relationship between the membrane potential (referred to by the authors as the postsynaptic input) and the neuron's spike timing using a linear function.  
This approximation enables the computation of gradients for weight updates.  
However, the method is limited to small learning rates, as the approximation error increases rapidly with higher learning rates~\cite{bohte}.


\textit{\ac{STDP}} refers to a class of learning rules inspired by biological neurons, in which synaptic weights are adjusted based on the timing of spike events.  
The core idea is that the synaptic weight connecting a pre- and postsynaptic neuron is modified according to the relative timing of their spikes~\cite{tavanaei_deep_2019}.  
\ac{STDP} is grounded in Donald Hebb’s 1949 neurobiological findings, which is why it is often referred to as a form of \textit{hebbian learning}~\cite{nunes_spiking_2022}.  
It can be summarized as follows~\cite{ponulak2_introduction_2011, nunes_spiking_2022}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If a postsynaptic spike follows a presynaptic spike, the synaptic weight is strengthened (potentiation).
    \item If a presynaptic spike follows a postsynaptic spike, the synaptic weight is weakened (depression).
\end{enumerate}
These rules are called \textit{hebbian}.  
Learning methods that reverse this temporal relationship (weakening the weight when the presynaptic spike follows the postsynaptic one) are known as \textit{anti-hebbian}~\cite{pietrzak_overview_2023}.  

Various equations exist to model weight updates in \acp{STDP}.  
Nunes et al. propose the following form for hebbian learning:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta w = \begin{cases}
        A e^{-\frac{t_{pre} - t_{post}}{\tau}}, & t_{pre} - t_{post} \le 0 \\
        B e^{-\frac{t_{pre} - t_{post}}{\tau}}, & t_{pre} - t_{post} > 0
    \end{cases}.
\end{equation}
\(\Delta w\) represents the weight change, \(A\) and \(B\) are learning rate parameters, and \(\tau\) is a time constant defining a temporal learning window.  
\(t_{pre}\) and \(t_{post}\) are point of times of the presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes.  
The upper case of the equation corresponds to weight strengthening and the lower case corresponds to weight weakening~\cite{nunes_spiking_2022}.

For example, Diehl and Cook successfully applied \ac{STDP} for unsupervised learning of the MNIST dataset.
They used a two-layered network architecture with \ac{LIF} neuron models, rate encoding and tested four different \ac{STDP} weight update rules.
The network with the best accuracy (ca. 95\%) consists of an input layer with 28x28 neurons and 6400 excitatory and 6400 inhibitory neurons~\cite{diehl_unsupervised_2015}.

In this section, \acp{SNN} and \acp{ANN} were compared in terms of their architectures and core functionalities.  
\acp{SNN} are considered more biologically plausible than their counterparts.  
However, this increased biological realism comes with higher complexity.

\section{\acp{SNN} on \acp{GPU}}

One major advantage of \acp{SNN} is their power efficiency when running on neuromorphic chips~\cite{tavanaei_deep_2019, Qu, pietrzak_overview_2023}.  
Neuromorphic chips use spikes to perform event-based computations that are aligned with the architecture of \acp{SNN}, consuming energy only when necessary~\cite{esser_backpropagation_2015}.  
For instance, Tang et al. demonstrated in the context of the \textit{simultaneous localization and mapping} problem (crucial task in robotics) that a \ac{SNN} running on Intel's neuromorphic Loihi chip achieved up to 100 times greater power efficiency than the widely used GMapping algorithm executed on a \ac{CPU}~\cite{Tang}.  

Despite these benefits, neuromorphic chips remain rare and not widely accessible~\cite{Qu}.  
This circumstance emphasizes the importance of developing efficient \ac{SNN} implementations on conventional hardware.  
Due to their high degree of parallelism, \acp{GPU} represent a promising platform for this purpose.

The further section is divided into two subcategories in which \ac{GPU} optimization approaches are explained:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low-level optimizations and
    \item \ac{SNN} frameworks with \ac{GPU} support.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Low-level optimizations for \ac{GPU} usage}
\textit{Low-level optimizations} refer to optimizations that exploit either the architecture of the \ac{GPU} or the structure of a \ac{SNN}, which leads to performance improvements or tackling hurdles independently from certain frameworks.
There are several examples in the literature, e.g. ~\cite{nageswaran_efficient_2009},~\cite{knight_efficient_2022}. 
This work focuses on the \textit{Temporal Fusion} method proposed by Li et al., which is a recent approach for optimizing the performance of \ac{LIF} neurons on single and multiple \acp{GPU}.
The authors base their method on the following iterative update rule for the \ac{LIF} neuron model:
\begin{equation}
    v_i^{(t)} = k_{\tau} \cdot v_i^{(t-1)} \cdot (1 - y_i^{(t-1)}) + V_{\text{rest}} \cdot y_i^{(t-1)} + x_i^{(t)}.
    \label{lilif}
\end{equation}
Here, \( v_i^{(t)} \) denotes the membrane potential of neuron \( i \) at time step \( t \), with initial condition \( v_i^{(0)} = V_{rest} \).
\( k_{\tau} \) is the decay factor, \( x_i^{(t)} \) represents the input from previous layers and \( y_i^{(t-1)} \) is an indicator function, denoting whether the neuron spiked at time step \( t-1 \).
A key observation is that each neuron depends only on its own state and certain inputs: its own previous output \( y_i^{(t-1)} \), current input \( x_i^{(t)} \) and membrane potential \( v_i^{(t-1)} \).
Under the assumption that all necessary inputs are available in advance for a given layer, the outputs of all neurons can be computed in parallel across time steps.
This applies not only to the forward pass, but also to the backward pass, which uses a surrogate gradient to address the non-differentiability of the spike function.
In practice, each neuron's computations are mapped to individual \ac{GPU} threads, and memory operations are fused across time steps to reduce overhead compared to sequential processing.
This method can be applied to single and multiple \acp{GPU} environments.
The authors show performance improvements (using CUDA and temporal fusion) up to 40 times regarding inference speed compared to snnTorch (a PyTorch-based \ac{SNN} framework) and 1.54 times compared to serial execution using CUDA without temporal fusion~\cite{li_towards_2024}.

\subsection{\ac{SNN} frameworks with \ac{GPU} support}
This section presents methods for running and training of \acp{SNN} on \acp{GPU} coupled to a certain framework.
The frameworks are categorized according to their primary focus: neuroscience or data science.  
This distinction is based on the observations of Garcia-Vico and Herrera.  
They note that most \ac{SNN} frameworks have traditionally been developed by neuroscientist interested in studying the behavior of biological neurons.  
As a result, the majority of existing frameworks emphasize the simulation of \acp{SNN}.  
In recent years, however, a growing demand for machine learning applications has led to the development of frameworks focusing on data science-related tasks~\cite{garcia-vico_preliminary_2021}.
In the following, a framework of each category is investigated.

\subsubsection{Brian: a neuroscience-focused framework} \label{sec:brian}
\textit{Brian/Brian2} is an open-source framework~\footnote{\url{https://briansimulator.org/}} for simulating \acp{SNN} with focus on neuroscience in Python.
First introduced by Goodman and Brette in 2008~\cite{goodman_brette}, it has been constantly maintained and was rewritten in 2019~\cite{stimberg_brette_goodman}.
At the core, Brian is equation based, which means, that neuron models are defined by writing their behavior as equations.
Brian focuses on flexibility regarding different synapses and neuron models making it highly suitable for neuroscientific experiments.
Although Brian's implementation was guided by efficiency on \ac{CPU}~\cite{stimberg_brette_goodman}, there are certain optimizations especially for \ac{GPU} usage.
This work explains Brian2GeNN~\cite{stimberg_brian2genn_2020} and on Brian2CUDA~\cite{alevi_brian2cuda_2022} more detailed, which are recent approaches for optimizing Brian's capabilities on \ac{GPU}.

\textit{Brian2GeNN} by Stimberg et al. connects the modeling capabilities of the Python-based Brian simulator with the GeNN framework, enabling \ac{GPU} acceleration without requiring users to write C++ or deeply understand \ac{GPU} architectures. 
GeNN is hereby a meta-compiler for generating C++ code for running \acp{SNN} on \acp{GPU}.
From the user's perspective, \ac{GPU} acceleration is activated by simply importing the Brian2GeNN package and setting execution to GeNN. 
Brian2GeNN executes the following pipeline steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Code generation and compilation
    \item Synapse creation and initialization
    \item Main simulation loop
    \item Set of smaller tasks like data format conversion and memory transfers.
\end{enumerate}
Initialization, code generation steps and the main simulation loop are executed on \ac{CPU}.
The core simulation is delegated to \ac{GPU}, where the generated code is executed.
After a simulation step, the data is copied back to the \ac{CPU} and converted.
This enables using Brian's monitoring capabilities~\cite{stimberg_brian2genn_2020}.

The authors benchmarked two networks with different neuron models.
For instance, a model with Hodgkin-Huxley neurons was tested, which reached speedups up to 26 times for over one million neurons using Tesla V100 compared to \ac{CPU} environment with 24 threads.
Across all tested conditions, \ac{GPU} execution was always faster than single-threaded CPU runs up to 400 times faster.
However, relative to the 24 threaded \ac{CPU} environment, speedups ranged between a factor of 2 times slower and 50 times faster, which indicates that speedups highly dependent on the underlying model architecture~\cite{stimberg_brian2genn_2020}. 

\textit{Brian2CUDA} by Alevi et al. extends the Brian simulator by introducing a \ac{GPU} backend that directly generates CUDA code from high-level Python model definitions, enabling parallel execution on NVIDIA \acp{GPU}. 
Activating \ac{GPU} execution is similar to the activation in Brian2GeNN by just adding two lines of code to a Brian script. 
The main difference between these two frameworks is, that Brian2CUDA has no restrictions regarding the feature set of Brian.
Brian2GeNN is restricted by the GeNN interface, which results in reduced capabilities compared to Brian2CUDA~\cite{alevi_brian2cuda_2022}.

Brian2CUDA defines three separate CUDA kernels, which run in a single thread for a specific neuron which allows parallelization across neurons~\cite{alevi_brian2cuda_2022}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item State integration kernel (handles neural state updates),
    \item Spike detection kernel (checks, whether a neural state is above an activation threshold),
    \item Reset state kernel (resets neurons which emitted spike).
\end{itemize}

Brian2CUDA has several optimizations, for example an optimization for handling global memory accesses:
Global memory access in CUDA kernels is optimized through coalesced access patterns, where consecutive threads access consecutive memory addresses.
This is important because CUDA transfers global memory in chunks of 32, 64, or 128 bytes.
When memory accesses are coalesced (meaning that the memory addresses are contiguous), all requested data can be transferred in a single operation.
This reduces the latency and increases throughput.
In contrast, non-coalesced access requires separate transfers per thread which is significantly slower~\cite{alevi_brian2cuda_2022}.

However, the simulation cannot be executed completely in parallel:
The spike detection requires atomic operations to increment shared spike counters and store IDs of neurons which emitted a spike.
Atomic operations are CUDA operations which can be safely called by multiple threads but only sequentially.
This introduces some serialization but remains efficient due to a typically low number of spikes per timestep~\cite{alevi_brian2cuda_2022}.  
 
Benchmarks demonstrate that Brian2CUDA outperforms Brian running on \ac{CPU} by up to three orders of magnitude for large-scale simulations.
It also surpasses Brian2GeNN but only for larger networks~\cite{alevi_brian2cuda_2022}. 

\subsubsection{Norse: a data science-focused framework}
\textit{Norse}\footnote{\url{https://norse.github.io/norse/}} is an open-source framework developed by Pehle and Pedersen~\cite{norse2021}, designed to facilitate machine learning tasks using \acp{SNN}~\cite{manna_frameworks_2023}.  
This is contrary to  traditional simulators such as Brian, which are optimized for neuroscientific modeling,
The framework is built on top of PyTorch, inheriting its native support for \ac{GPU} acceleration~\cite{manna_frameworks_2023, garcia-vico_preliminary_2021}.  
Users can activate CUDA support by adding \textit{.to('cuda')} to declared PyTorch models~\cite{norse2021}, which simplifies \ac{GPU} usage.
Supervised learning in Norse is implemented via the SuperSpike algorithm~\cite{manna_frameworks_2023, garcia-vico_preliminary_2021}
This is a surrogate gradient method that enables gradient-based learning in \acp{SNN}~\cite{zenke_superspike_2018}.  
In addition to SuperSpike, Manna et al. mention support for \ac{STDP} ~\cite{manna_frameworks_2023}.
However, they do not clarify whether the \ac{STDP} implementation in Norse is optimized for or compatible with \ac{GPU} execution.  
Furthermore, there are no benchmarks regarding performance of Norse comparing \ac{CPU} and \ac{GPU}.

This section introduced concepts and frameworks for running \acp{SNN} on \acp{GPU}.
While neuromorphic hardware remains the ideal platform for maximizing energy efficiency and performance, recent developments demonstrate the growing maturity of \ac{SNN} tooling on common hardware like \acp{GPU}.
